# Economic Terms and Concepts

## Business Cycle Phases

**Economic Cycle**
- The cyclical pattern of economic growth and contraction that occurs in all market economies
- Consists of four main phases: expansion, peak, contraction, and trough
- Driven by changes in aggregate demand, supply, investment, and consumer spending
- Average cycle lasts 5-10 years, but can vary significantly
- Understanding cycles helps predict CEO performance and company resilience

**Expansion**
- Phase when the economy is growing and GDP is increasing
- Characterized by rising employment, increasing consumer spending, and business investment
- Stock markets typically perform well during expansions as company profits rise
- Consumers have more confidence and are more likely to spend
- Companies often hire more employees and invest in growth during expansions
- Can lead to inflation if growth becomes too rapid

**Contraction (Recession)**
- Phase when the economy is shrinking and GDP is declining
- Defined as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth
- Characterized by rising unemployment, reduced consumer spending, and business cutbacks
- Stock markets typically decline as company profits fall
- Companies may lay off employees and reduce investments
- Consumer confidence drops and spending slows significantly

**Recession**
- A significant, widespread, and prolonged downturn in economic activity
- Formal definition: two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth
- Can last 6 months to 2+ years depending on severity
- Notable recessions: 2001, 2008-2009, 2020
- During recessions, CEO performance often varies - some CEOs cut costs effectively, others fail to adapt
- Stock performance typically negative, but varies by company and sector

**Trough**
- The lowest point in the economic cycle before recovery begins
- Point where GDP stops falling and starts growing again
- Often marks the beginning of the next expansion phase
- Economic indicators start improving (unemployment may still be high but hiring begins)

**Peak**
- The highest point in the economic cycle before contraction begins
- Point where GDP stops growing and starts declining
- Often marked by tight labor markets and rising inflation
- May signal upcoming interest rate increases by central banks

## Market Conditions

**Bull Market**
- Sustained period of rising stock prices (typically 20%+ increase from recent lows)
- Characterized by investor optimism and economic growth
- Companies perform well and CEO transitions often have positive impacts
- Generally lasts months to years
- Public confidence is high, consumer spending increases

**Bear Market**
- Sustained period of declining stock prices (typically 20%+ decrease from recent highs)
- Characterized by investor pessimism and economic weakness
- Companies struggle and CEO transitions face headwinds
- Generally lasts months to years
- Public confidence is low, consumer spending decreases

## Economic Indicators

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**
- Total value of goods and services produced by a country in a specific period
- Usually measured quarterly or annually
- Growth rate shows whether economy is expanding or contracting
- Positive GDP growth = expansion; negative = contraction
- Key metric for overall economic health

**Unemployment Rate**
- Percentage of people actively looking for work but without jobs
- Rises during recessions, falls during expansions
- Affects consumer spending and company labor costs
- High unemployment = economic stress; low unemployment = strong economy

**Inflation**
- General increase in prices of goods and services over time
- Measured as percentage change year-over-year
- Moderate inflation (2-3%) is healthy; high inflation (>5%) is problematic
- Central banks typically raise interest rates to combat high inflation
- Inflation affects CEO decisions on pricing and investment

**Interest Rates**
- Rate at which central banks lend money to commercial banks
- Affects borrowing costs for companies and consumers
- Rising rates discourage borrowing and spending (contractionary)
- Falling rates encourage borrowing and spending (expansionary)
- CEO financing decisions heavily influenced by interest rate environment

## Index and Benchmark Terms

**S&P 100 (OEX) Index**
- Index of 100 largest U.S. companies by market capitalization
- Used as benchmark for company performance comparison
- All companies in this analysis are part of S&P 100
- Helps contextualize individual company performance vs. broad market
- More focused than S&P 500 (larger companies only)

**Recession Period / Recession Benchmark**
- Period when economy contracted (negative GDP growth)
- Key recessions: 2001 (post-9/11), 2008-2009 (financial crisis), 2020 (COVID-19)
- Benchmark shows how CEO performance compared to S&P 100 average during recession
- Companies often show different resilience during recessions
- Some sectors more affected than others during recessions

**Outperformance / Relative Performance**
- How a company performed vs. the broader market during same period
- Example: company down 10% while S&P 100 down 15% = +5% outperformance
- Shows whether CEO navigated economic challenges better than peers
- Important metric during recession periods

## Impact on CEO Performance and Sentiment

**CEO Performance During Expansions**
- Generally easier to show positive returns
- Growing economy provides tailwind for companies
- Investors more forgiving of mistakes
- Stock gains often attributed to economic tailwinds, not CEO skill
- New CEOs can easily achieve positive returns in expansion periods
- Harder to judge true capability without economic adversity

**CEO Performance During Contractions/Recessions**
- Much harder to generate positive returns
- Economic headwinds require CEO skill to navigate
- Cost-cutting and operational efficiency become critical
- Strong CEOs stand out as they preserve shareholder value in downturns
- Weak CEOs are quickly exposed when forced to make tough decisions
- May be replaced rapidly if unable to manage crisis

**Investor Sentiment Classification**
- Positive Investor Reaction: Stock gained >2% (optimistic about new CEO)
- Negative Investor Reaction: Stock fell <-2% (concerned about new leadership)
- Neutral Investor Reaction: Stock moved between -2% and +2% (ordinary change)
- Sentiment influenced by: company health, economic conditions, CEO track record
- Can shift as market learns about new CEO's strategy and execution

**Market Expectations vs. Actual Results**
- If pre-transition trend was negative but stock recovered under new CEO = positive surprise
- If pre-transition trend was positive but stock declined = disappointing performance
- Market reacts to unexpected changes more dramatically
- Strong performance in expansion is less impressive than in recession

## Sector Analysis and Comparison

**Sector-Specific Performance**
- Different sectors respond differently to CEO changes
- Technology: high growth expectations, volatile stock movement
- Financials: heavily influenced by interest rates and economic cycles
- Energy: influenced by commodity prices and global conditions
- Healthcare: less cyclical, more stable performance
- Consumer Discretionary: highly sensitive to economic cycles

**Sector Mean and Outlier Analysis**
- Sector mean shows average CEO impact across industry
- Outliers (top/bottom 20%) stand out from their peers
- Comparing CEO to sector mean shows relative performance
- Statistical methodology uses z-scores to identify outliers
- Sector analysis controls for industry-specific factors

**Long-Tenure vs. Short-Tenure CEOs**
- Long-tenure CEOs: exceptional leaders who create sustained value
- Short-tenure CEOs: may indicate instability or poor board fit
- Percentile comparison shows how tenure compares to sector average
- Tenure doesn't guarantee success, but shows investor confidence
